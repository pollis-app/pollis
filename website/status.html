Status

What the services are doing, what has gone wrong since this record opened, and what an outage actually costs on a system whose retention is bounded by device check-ins.

The health and build endpoints below have been open to anyone for months, and until now nothing published them. That left the worst possible gap on a product like this: during an outage there was no way to tell a broken service from a seized one, and no way to tell either from nothing having happened at all. Silence is the same shape as a compromise.

So this page publishes observations rather than assurances. Every endpoint listed is one you can query yourself — the commands are at the bottom, and none of them needs anything from us.

2 · Why an outage here is a correctness question

On most services, availability and correctness are separate concerns: it is down, then it is up, and nothing was lost in between. Pollis does not get to say that, and the reason is worth understanding before reading anything else on this page.

An encrypted message waiting on the server is kept until every current member device has reported collecting it — bounded by the slowest device, never by a clock. The single bound on that is on devices: a device that has not checked in for twelve months stops holding mail back for itself, and mail waiting only on it becomes collectable. That bound is keyed on the device's last report, and a device that cannot reach the Delivery Service cannot report.

So an outage does not merely make Pollis unavailable — it spends the check-in budget of every device that is offline for its duration. An hour is nowhere near twelve months and costs nothing real. A fault that stopped a class of devices reporting for months would be a different thing entirely, and it would be recorded here as affecting message delivery, in those words, rather than filed as downtime and forgotten once the service came back.

Every incident in the record below therefore answers that question explicitly, as a published field, whichever way the answer goes. The full rule is in the incident-record format, and the retention rule it depends on is on the retention page.

3 · Incidents

Recorded to a written format with a severity ladder and a published deadline: a postmortem within 14 days for anything that stopped people sending or receiving, and for anything that touched message delivery or the transparency log. That deadline is enforced by a check in CI, not by memory — the build fails when a postmortem comes due and is not there.

4 · The transparency log, and the stall nobody can see

The three signed Merkle trees are republished by a daily job. That creates a failure mode specific to this design, and it is worth stating on the page rather than in an engineering document: a stalled log and a withheld log look identical from outside.

A Signed Tree Head carries a timestamp, but that timestamp is frozen per tree size — an unchanged tree is republished with the head it already had, so the head for a given size is byte-stable forever. An old timestamp is therefore exactly what a healthy idle log looks like, and exactly what a log nobody is publishing any more looks like. You cannot tell them apart from the served files.

What a monitor should conclude

A gap is not evidence of honesty or of tampering — it is evidence of nothing, and should be treated as such. If the publisher has not run for more than about two days, the correct reading is "this log is not currently being published", not "the log is fine, it is just quiet". The check that catches equivocation is different work again: run pollis-verify yourself and keep the heads you saw, so that a tree which later disagrees with what you were served is provable rather than suspected. The artifacts page has those commands.

The remaining hole in that story is that nobody else currently keeps those heads for you. The whitepaper states it plainly: Pollis runs the only log and the only first-party auditor, and no third party is contractually watching yet. The defence is that the verifier is released so anyone can be that third party; the monitoring that would detect a second, well-signed tree without you is designed and unbuilt (issue #763). It is named here rather than papered over.

5 · Recorded observations

A sample is recorded when something changes, and otherwise every 20 hours as a heartbeat. The heartbeat is the load-bearing part: without it, "no new entries" and "the monitoring is dead" would look the same, which is the exact confusion this page exists to remove.

6 · Check it yourself

Nothing on this page is privileged. These are the same requests the prober makes, and they need no credentials:

curl -sS https://api.pollis.com/health Returns ok. This is a liveness probe and nothing more — it answers without touching the database, so a healthy ok is compatible with a service that cannot actually read or write. It is reported here as what it is rather than dressed up as a health check. curl -sS https://api.pollis.com/version The git commit the running Delivery Service was built from. This is the useful one: it is how a deploy is confirmed to be live rather than merely built, and comparing it against the public commit history tells you what code is answering you. curl -sS https://verify.pollis.com/v1/sth/latest.json The signed head of the MLS commit-log tree. Also /v1/account-keys/sth/latest.json and /v1/binaries/sth/latest.json. Verifying a head, rather than merely fetching one, is what pollis-verify is for — see artifacts. curl -sS https://cdn.pollis.com/releases/latest.json The release manifest the download buttons and the install scripts read.

The record this page renders is website/status-history.json, appended by scripts/status-probe.sh and committed. Every sample ever taken is in that file's git history, so an entry that was later softened is a visible diff rather than a silent edit.

Where this page's guarantee ends

It is a sample, not a monitor. One request per endpoint, every 30 minutes, from a single GitHub-hosted runner in one network location, on a scheduler that is routinely late and occasionally skips runs. A short outage between samples leaves no trace here. Read it as "this is what was observed at these times", never as an uptime percentage — which is why no uptime percentage is quoted.

Your view is not this view. A regional network problem between you and Cloudflare is invisible to a probe that does not share your path.

The Delivery Service scales to zero. It runs as a single container that sleeps after ten minutes idle, so the first request after a quiet period pays a cold start. A slow response in the table above is usually that, not a fault.

We operate the prober. This is a first-party page reporting on first-party services, and that is a real limit: an operator who stopped publishing would produce a page that simply stopped updating. Two things push back on it — the record is committed to a public repository, where a gap and an edit are both visible in history, and a separate daily job fails the build when the newest sample is more than 48 hours old, so a stopped prober is caught by something other than the prober. Neither makes this page a substitute for checking the endpoints yourself.

The relay overlay is not probed here. Its nodes are deliberately short-lived and rotate between regions, so a fixed list of hosts would be wrong within the day. Its published directory is signed and independently checkable — see subprocessors.

This page can lag main. Publishing the site is a deliberate manual act (so that main stays always-deployable), which means a recorded sample or a filed incident is not visible here until the site is deployed. A daily check asserts that what pollis.com serves still matches what the repository says.
