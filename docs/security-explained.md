How Pollis Keeps Your Conversations Private

Pollis is a chat app for groups, on desktop and mobile. If you have used Slack, Discord or Microsoft Teams, you know the shape: groups, channels inside groups, direct messages, voice, and files. The difference is what happens to a message after it leaves your device. Slack, Teams and Discord can read every message you send, because their servers store them in plaintext. Pollis's servers cannot, because a message is encrypted on your device before it is sent and only the people you sent it to can decrypt it. This document explains how that works without assuming a computer-science background, and names the cryptography involved so you can look it up.

The first question in security is who you have to trust. With most messaging apps you trust your device, the company that built the app, and the company that runs the servers. With Pollis you trust your device and the version of the app you installed. You do not have to trust the servers: the database, file storage, voice routing and the email service that sends your sign-in code are all assumed to be watched by people you have never met. Everything sensitive happens on your device. Messages are encrypted there, and your private keys are generated there and never leave it in readable form. The servers see who is in which group, who talks to whom, and when, much as a mail carrier sees the addresses on envelopes. They do not see what is inside.

Pollis also limits what those addresses reveal. The stored copy of each message used to record who sent it; now it holds a meaningless placeholder, and the recipient learns the sender from a signature sealed inside the message. If the message database were stolen or subpoenaed, it would not show who said what. The limit is that this protects stored records, not the moment of sending: the delivery service still checks that you are a member of the conversation when you send, and that check identifies you. Removing that requires a further upgrade, anonymous membership proofs, which is not built. Text messages are also padded to a few fixed sizes before encryption, so their length gives little away (files are not padded, because the way they are shared depends on their real size). The real-time "new message" ping no longer names the sender. On phones, notifications carry no sender, no content and not even which conversation they are about: just a random one-time code the app exchanges with Pollis, over its own encrypted connection, to find out where to go.

The encryption for conversations is MLS, Messaging Layer Security, a public standard published in 2023 as RFC 9420. It was designed for groups that change over time, where people join and leave and use several devices, and it keeps working for very large groups. Wire and Cisco Webex use it too. Pollis uses the OpenMLS library, written in Rust. An MLS group runs on a bundle of algorithms called a cipher suite. Pollis's suite combines four kinds of pieces: a way for devices to agree on a shared secret, a function that shapes that secret into keys (HKDF-SHA384), a cipher that scrambles the message and detects tampering (ChaCha20-Poly1305), and a digital signature that proves which device sent something.

The key agreement is built to resist future quantum computers. The usual algorithm, X25519, is secure against every computer that exists today, but a large enough quantum computer would break it. Nobody knows whether such a machine will be built. What is certain is that an adversary can record encrypted traffic now and keep it, betting the machine arrives before the contents stop mattering. This is called "harvest now, decrypt later." So Pollis pairs X25519 with ML-KEM-768, which NIST standardized in 2024 as FIPS 203, using a combination called X-Wing. The shared secret stays safe as long as either algorithm holds, so an attacker has to break both. ML-KEM is new enough that keeping the well-tested classical algorithm alongside it is cheap insurance.

Signatures are quantum-resistant too, though for a different reason. A signature only needs to hold up when it is checked; a future quantum computer cannot go back and forge a message that was already delivered and accepted. But some signatures are meant to stay checkable for years. Your account identity key is checked by every device that ever admits one of your devices into a conversation. A device certificate is re-checked each time that device appears somewhere new. And the public audit log described below exists so that its record of the past can be verified indefinitely; if its signatures became forgeable, a future operator could rewrite history in a way that still checked out. So Pollis signs with ML-DSA-44, NIST's FIPS 204, for account keys, device certificates, requests to the delivery service, the audit log, and every message signature inside a group. The cost is size: a public key is 1312 bytes instead of 32, and a signature 2420 bytes instead of 64, roughly eight times more identity data on every device record, group change and server request. Private keys did not grow, so how your keys are stored did not change.

Pollis originally ran a classical cipher suite (X25519, AES-128-GCM, Ed25519) and later added the quantum-resistant one alongside it. The classical suite has since been retired. MLS does not let a running group change suites, so Pollis moves a group by creating a successor group on the new suite and moving every member across in one step. Before it creates anything, it reserves a slot in the new group for every device on the member list, and it abandons the move if even one is missing: everyone crosses or nobody does. The migration machinery stays in place because the new suite's official number is still provisional and could change. The limit is that this only protects the future: messages sealed under the classical suite before a group moved stay that way.

Pollis has three layers of identity. The first is your account identity: one long-lived signing key, created on the device where you signed up. Its private half is a 32-byte secret that never leaves your devices in readable form; its public half is published so others can check your signatures. The second is the device: each device has its own identifier and its own signing keys. The third is your device's position, called a leaf, inside each group's encrypted state. Each leaf is signed by the device's key, and the device's keys are signed by your account key. That chain is what stops a malicious server from slipping a fake device into your account: the fake has no signature from your account key, so honest devices refuse to add it, and if a misbehaving member adds it anyway, every other member notices, records it, and removes it.

On top of that, Pollis borrows safety numbers from Signal. Each pair of people has a sixty-digit number derived from both account keys, shown as twelve blocks of five digits, with a QR code to scan. If you and the other person see the same number, nobody, including someone with full control of Pollis's servers, has swapped their key. Until you compare, your device remembers each person's key the first time it sees it ("trust on first use"). If the key later changes, a red shield appears next to that person everywhere they show up, along with a notice in the chat, so you can re-check before trusting the conversation. Verification is per person: compare once and the green shield follows them across direct messages, member lists and channels.

Signing in works differently from most apps. There is no password, because passwords get reused, phished and leaked. The first time you sign in on a device, Pollis emails you a six-digit code, which proves you control the address. After that, on that device, you use a four-digit PIN instead. The PIN never leaves the device and Pollis has no record of it. It unlocks the keys stored in your operating system's keychain, which open your local message database and sign on your behalf. Without the PIN, someone looking inside the keychain sees only scrambled bytes.

Four digits are enough because of Argon2id, the recommended way to turn a short secret into something hard to guess. Pollis tunes it to use 64 megabytes of memory and about a quarter of a second per attempt, so an attacker with a stolen copy of your keystore pays that cost for each of the ten thousand possible PINs. After ten wrong attempts on the device, the encrypted keys are deleted. The keys themselves are wrapped with XChaCha20-Poly1305, a cipher whose long random nonce rules out a class of rare implementation mistakes.

The PIN protects the device; the Secret Key protects the account. At signup Pollis shows you a code that starts with A3 followed by six groups of five characters. The characters come from a 32-symbol alphabet that drops I, L, O and U because they look like 1 and 0. Thirty random characters give 150 bits of entropy, far beyond what anyone could guess. The server keeps a copy of your account key, locked in a box that only your Secret Key opens (HKDF-SHA256 to derive the unlocking key, AES-256-GCM to unlock). Pollis shows you the Secret Key once and does not keep it. If you lose it and every device you were signed in on, your account cannot be recovered, because nobody else holds the key.

To add a device, you can approve it from one you already use. The new device creates a temporary key pair and turns its public half into an eight-character code, which it displays. Your existing device shows an empty box and asks you to type that code. It deliberately does not show the code itself, because a screen that displays a code and accepts it with one tap is a comparison nobody actually made. Your existing device works out the code independently from the temporary key it received and checks what you typed against it. If they match, the two devices run X25519 key agreement, producing a shared secret only they know even though the exchange passed through the server, and use it (via HKDF-SHA256 and AES-256-GCM) to hand over your account key. Because the code is computed from the temporary key, an attacker who swaps in their own key changes the code, and the two screens disagree. Finding a different key that produces the same eight characters takes about a trillion attempts, and the work cannot be reused across requests. The other route is recovery with your Secret Key. Either way, the new device sets a PIN, publishes a device certificate signed by your account key, and joins each of your groups using a published snapshot of the group, an MLS feature called external commit. New devices start empty: they do not receive your old messages.

Data on your device is encrypted too. The local database, which holds decrypted messages and group state, uses SQLCipher, a version of SQLite that encrypts every page with AES-256 and protects it against tampering with HMAC-SHA512. Its key is 32 random bytes, stored only in the keystore and only encrypted under your PIN. A copy of your disk taken while the app is locked is unreadable. Your contact list and group list are not stored on disk at all; they are fetched through Pollis's delivery service when needed. Your device holds no password to the server's database, so a stolen device cannot dump your social graph either. Windows reaches the same result by a different route, because the usual cryptography library conflicts there with the one the video stack brings; it uses the Rust cryptography libraries the app already carries, with the same format and algorithms. Automated tests on Windows, macOS and Linux write a message, read the database file back byte by byte, and confirm the message is not in it and that neither no key nor a wrong key opens it.

Files use convergent encryption. The app computes a SHA-256 fingerprint of the file, derives the encryption key from that fingerprint with HKDF-SHA256, and encrypts the file with AES-256-GCM in four-megabyte chunks so large files never have to sit in memory at once. The encrypted file is stored in Cloudflare R2 under its fingerprint, so two people uploading the same file share one stored copy. The cost is that someone who already has a candidate file can compute its fingerprint and check whether anyone has uploaded it. They cannot read anything, but they can confirm a file's presence. MEGA makes the same trade. Pollis accepts it for the storage savings; per-conversation keys would remove it at the cost of deduplication.

Voice is end-to-end encrypted as well. Calls go through a LiveKit server that forwards each person's audio to the others. The connection to that server uses DTLS-SRTP like every WebRTC app, but that encryption ends at the server, which is why services like Slack Huddles and Google Meet can technically listen in. Pollis adds a second layer: each audio frame is encrypted with AES-128-GCM on your device, after compression and before it is sent, using libwebrtc's FrameCryptor. The server still routes the packets but cannot open them. Discord's DAVE protocol, launched in 2024, works the same way. The voice key comes from the channel's MLS group: Pollis exports a 32-byte secret labelled pollis/voice/v1 for the current epoch, so every member derives the same key and the server never sees it. When someone joins or leaves, the group moves to a new epoch and the key rotates mid-call without anyone reconnecting; people who left cannot decrypt what follows. Encryption is always on. Voice is a desktop feature; the mobile app does not do calls.

There are limits worth stating plainly. Pollis can see which conversations exist, who is in them, and roughly when they are busy; a server that delivers messages needs that. An optional relay mode can hide your internet address from Pollis's servers, but it is off by default. If someone takes your device while it is unlocked, they can read your messages. Your privacy also depends on the people you talk to. Healing after a device compromise is driven by the app running, not a timer: your device refreshes its keys in each group when it joins and then, at startup, whenever its keys in a group are about a week old, so a group whose members are all offline for a month does not heal during that month. There is no backup of message history: a device you add later cannot read anything from before it joined, because MLS's forward secrecy makes that impossible without a backup, and Pollis deliberately has none. If you forget your PIN and lose your Secret Key, the account is gone. These are trade-offs that favour privacy where it conflicts with convenience.

You can also check the app itself. Everything above assumes the copy of Pollis on your device is the honest one. A code signature only proves that Pollis's signing key produced the file; it would not stop a build made to spy on one person. So Pollis records a fingerprint of every release in a public, append-only log at verify.pollis.com, alongside the logs of account keys and group history. Anyone can run pollis-verify release to confirm a version is in that log, trusting only a fixed public key, and the Security page in the app can check the running build. A secretly altered build would either appear in the log permanently or be visibly missing from it. Releases also carry signatures recorded in a public log Pollis does not run (Sigstore's Rekor), tied to the build system that produced them. For Linux, an independent rebuild from the public source code runs after every release and confirms the result matches the published fingerprint byte for byte. For macOS and Windows, anyone can check that the published files match the log, but rebuilding them identically from source is not yet possible.

Compared with apps you may know: Signal and WhatsApp encrypt messages end to end with the Signal Protocol (X3DH and the Double Ratchet, from 2014); Pollis uses MLS, which suits large groups and multiple devices better. Slack and Teams do not encrypt messages end to end; enterprise key-management options control encryption at rest, but the server can still read content. Discord's chat is readable by the server, but its DAVE protocol encrypts voice and video, as Pollis does for voice. Element and Matrix use Megolm, which gives up some of MLS's post-compromise security in exchange for server-side history backup, which Pollis does not offer. iMessage encrypts per device rather than per group, and backs up to iCloud under Apple's keys unless you enable Advanced Data Protection. Wire is the closest cryptographic relative, also using MLS through OpenMLS, though Pollis has moved to the quantum-resistant suite. In product shape, Pollis is closest to Slack, except that the server cannot read your messages.

In short: Pollis encrypts your messages on your device with MLS before sending them, using keys only your devices hold. Key agreement combines a classical and a quantum-resistant algorithm, so recording traffic today for a future quantum computer does not help. Signatures are quantum-resistant too, at the cost of size. Your local data is encrypted under a key that is itself locked by your PIN. Your account can move to a new device only through one of your existing devices or your 150-bit Secret Key. Files and voice are encrypted before they leave your device.
