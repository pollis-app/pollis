Pollis is an app for sending messages — one-on-one, in a small group, or in larger spaces with channels. The thing that makes it different is that we, the people running Pollis, can't read what you write. Most messaging apps could if they wanted to. We can't.

Think of a sealed envelope, but one nobody can steam open. Before your message leaves your device, it's locked so that only the people you're writing to can open it. We can't. Even if our servers were broken into, the messages would stay unreadable. Groups work the same way: only the people currently in a group can open its messages. Someone who joins later can read what's said from the moment they joined onward, but not what came before. That's on purpose.

The locks are built to survive a machine that doesn't exist yet. Someone could record encrypted traffic today and wait for a future quantum computer to crack it. Pollis locks each message with two mechanisms at once, the well-tested one everyone uses and a newer one designed to resist quantum computers, so an attacker would have to break both. Every new group starts with both locks.

We also keep the outside of the envelope as blank as we can. The stored copy of a message doesn't say who sent it; only the people in the conversation can tell, from inside. If our database were stolen or handed over, it wouldn't show who said what. Text messages are padded to standard sizes, so their length gives little away. The notification our servers send to your phone doesn't say who wrote, what they wrote, or even which conversation it's in. Your phone fetches the message and unlocks it itself, so by default it can show you the sender and a preview; a setting turns that off.

If someone's key ever changes without warning, which is what an attacker on our servers would try, Pollis notices. A red shield appears next to their name everywhere you see them, with a notice in the chat, so you can check with them directly. You only verify a person once; the green shield then follows them everywhere.

To use Pollis you sign in with your email (we send you a one-time code) and pick a four-digit PIN. The PIN unlocks the app on that device, and after ten wrong guesses the device deletes the keys that open your messages there. You also get a long backup code. Keep it somewhere safe: a password manager, or paper in a drawer. If you lose your devices, or forget your PIN with no other device still signed in to approve a new one, that code is the only way back in. We don't keep a copy.

You can use Pollis on more than one device. Sign in on the new one, type the code it shows into the old one, and the two agree privately to trust each other. New devices start empty, but you can copy your old messages across from the old device: they go straight from one of your devices to the other, sealed so we can't read them, once you approve it there with your PIN. Both receive everything from then on.

Files like pictures, documents and voice notes are sealed the same way. So is voice chat on desktop: your audio is sealed on your computer before it reaches our voice servers, which pass along sound they can't listen to. The same group keys that protect your messages produce the voice key, so the people in the channel can hear you and nobody else can.

Some trade-offs. Because we can't read your messages, we can't recover them if you lose everything. We can still see which conversations exist, who's in them, and roughly when they're busy; delivering messages requires that. If someone takes your device while it's unlocked, they can read your messages. And your privacy is only as strong as the people you're talking to.

You can also check the app itself. We publish a fingerprint of every release in a public list that can't be quietly edited, and anyone can confirm the app they're running is on it. So we can't slip one person a tampered copy without it showing up in that list forever or being obviously missing. On every platform, the list proves you have the copy we published, and for Linux anyone can also rebuild the app from the public source code and confirm it matches byte for byte.

That's the whole picture. It works like a normal messaging app, except we built it so we couldn't snoop even if we wanted to or were forced to. The cost is that you have to keep your PIN and backup code safe: there's no help desk that can let you back in.
